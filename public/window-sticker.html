What the original window sticker was

Every new car sold in the United States has to carry a Monroney label — the paper sticker taped to the glass listing the model, the engine, the standard equipment, the options the dealer added and the price of each. It's named after Senator Mike Monroney, whose 1958 law made it illegal to sell a new car without one. Once the car is driven off the lot that sticker is usually thrown away, and with it the cleanest single summary of how the vehicle was built.

This tool reconstructs the part of that label that lives permanently inside the VIN. Feed it 17 characters and it returns the model year, make, model, trim, body style, engine, fuel type, drivetrain, transmission and the plant that assembled the car, presented in sticker layout so it reads the way the original did.

What it can't rebuild

Be clear on the limits before you rely on it: original MSRP, individual option packages and dealer-installed extras are held in manufacturer systems, not encoded in the VIN, so no VIN-based tool can recover them. What you get here is the factory build sheet — accurate, permanent, and enough to confirm a listing's trim and engine claims are real.

And build data says nothing about what happened afterward. Accidents, branded titles, odometer rollbacks and skipped service are recorded across a car's life, which is why they only appear in a full vehicle history report.
